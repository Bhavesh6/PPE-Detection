Three seconds at the gate

No new habits to learn, no forms, nothing to remember. A worker does what they already do: turn up.

01

They badge in

A card touch identifies who is at the gate. The screen greets them by name — not an ID number.

02

The camera looks

A vision model checks for a hardhat and a high-visibility vest, and holds the answer for a moment to be sure.

03

The gate answers

Cleared, and they are marked present. Missing something, and the screen names it so they can fix it and scan again.

One word, readable across the yard

These are real screens, not mock-ups — the detection boxes and the ruling come from the model itself.

It tells them what to fix

A refusal that just says “no” teaches nobody anything. This one names the missing item, in plain words, at a size readable from across the gate — then clears itself so the queue keeps moving.

Every decision is written down against a name, so a supervisor asked what controls were in place has an answer made of records rather than recollection. A refusal keeps the frame that produced it — the model's own boxes, burned in — so the answer isn't just a claim, it's a photograph.

It checks what the law already asks for

The BOCW Act, the Factories Act and the Occupational Safety Code already oblige an employer to supply protective equipment free of cost. Helmet, safety shoes and reflective jacket are the baseline on an Indian site, and the equipment must be ISI-certified to BIS standards.

Nothing here invents a policy. It verifies an existing one at the only moment that matters — the moment somebody walks in.

Round one to round two

Round one was a detector in a browser tab, and it ended on a slide promising a checkpoint. Round two is that checkpoint, measured against the promise, including where it falls short.

Round one

  • YOLOv8 reading protective equipment from any webcam
  • A dashboard that logged violations with a timestamp, and spoke the alert aloud
  • Inference on a hosted GPU, frontend on Vercel
  • One shared session — every visitor saw the same counters

It watched, and it told you. It could not stop anybody, and it did not know who it was looking at. Round one source →

Round two

  • A badge-operated checkpoint that decides entry and writes it down
  • Named workers, not anonymous frames, with attendance on the record
  • An eight-page console for the people who answer for the site
  • A safety policy that changes at runtime, never by redeploy
  • Hazard alerts that can stop the gate everywhere at once
  • A native gate application for the Raspberry Pi
  • An append-only record of who changed what, and when

What round one promised, marked honestly

The last slide of the round-one deck listed what would come next. This is that list, against what is actually running.

Built
An MFRC522 reader over SPI, used to identify the person rather than the equipment. The camera was already better at the equipment.

Predictive analytics on risk

Multi-site central dashboard

Supervisor app with push notifications
Not yet

Dropped

The decision
  • Badge scan identifies the worker, then the camera rules on their equipment
  • A pass has to hold for over a second, so one lucky frame cannot clear anybody
  • Entry is marked against a name, refusals included
  • Model speed shown live, in frames per second, on the gate view
Policy
  • Required equipment and detection confidence are settings, not constants
  • A change takes effect on the next frame, across all three surfaces
  • Only equipment the model can actually see is offered, because a requirement it could never satisfy would refuse everyone, permanently
Evidence
  • Every refusal keeps the frame that caused it, with the model's own boxes drawn in
  • Images expire after thirty days; the decision they support does not
  • Served through an authenticated route, never as a public file
  • Operators can capture a frame on demand, kept out of the compliance figures
Console
  • Overview, personnel, captures, reports, analytics, policy, location, change log, alerts
  • Per-worker scorecards, daily trend, and the hours refusals cluster in
  • Named CSV exports for any date range
Hazard alerts
  • A critical alert overrides equipment compliance and pauses entry. Full protective gear is still not safe in a gas leak
  • Raised either by a device declaring a severity, or by a raw sensor value crossing a threshold an administrator set
  • Surfaces on the gate display, the device, and every open console tab
  • Clearing one is recorded against the person who cleared it
Accountability
  • An append-only log across policy, personnel and alerts
  • Names are kept alongside ids, so the trail still reads after an account is deleted
  • Changes that weaken the gate are marked as such
The device
  • A fullscreen gate application that owns the camera and badge reader directly
  • Decisions that cannot reach the server are held locally and retried, not lost
  • Site position reported from the device, or set by hand until a module exists
  • A pre-flight tool that names the broken link instead of leaving a blank screen
Interface
  • Rebuilt around one editorial design language, shared by every page
  • A full-site layout audit: four real bugs found where content overflowed with no way to scroll to it
  • Every page checked at phone, tablet and desktop widths

What is not done

  • No hardware has been tested. The badge reader, GPS module and battery backup all have working code and none of it has met the real component yet.
  • Inference runs on the server, not on the AI accelerator. That swap is waiting on a measurement, not a decision.
  • The model knows three things: hardhat, safety vest, mask. Not gloves, not boots; those need a retrained model.
  • There is no exit scan, so a head count means “admitted today”, not “still inside”. It is shown as the former.
  • The sensor board is not built. Alerts are exercised through a simulator that calls the same endpoint the board will.

Decisions we would defend

Refusals are photographed. Grants are not.
A checkpoint that photographs everyone is a surveillance system with a safety feature attached.
Making the gate weaker is as visible as making it stronger.
Configurable policy without a log would make every compliance figure downstream unfalsifiable.
The offline queue does not deduplicate.
A decision written twice is untidy. A decision lost is the thing the queue exists to prevent.
Any signed-in user can clear a hazard alert.
It is a safety action, not a policy edit. A gas alarm should not wait for an administrator to be reachable.
